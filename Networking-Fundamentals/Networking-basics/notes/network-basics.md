What I Learned

In this phase, I built a solid foundation in computer networking by learning what a network is, why networks exist, and how they make 
communication between devices possible. I understood that a network is simply a collection of devices connected together so they can 
exchange data and share resources such as files, printers, and internet access.

I learned that networks are categorized based on the area they cover. A Personal Area Network (PAN) connects personal devices over a very 
short distance, such as a phone and wireless earbuds. A Local Area Network (LAN) connects devices within a small area like a home, office, 
or university. A Metropolitan Area Network (MAN) links multiple LANs across a city, while a Wide Area Network (WAN) connects networks over 
large geographical areas, making the internet the world's largest WAN.

I also studied the main networking devices and their purposes. I learned that a modem establishes communication between my network and my 
Internet Service Provider (ISP), a router directs traffic between different networks and ensures data reaches the correct destination, and 
a switch connects devices within the same local network, allowing them to communicate efficiently. I also became familiar with other 
devices such as wireless access points, hubs, bridges, repeaters, and network interface cards (NICs).

Another important topic was network topologies, which describe how devices are arranged and connected. I explored star, bus, ring, mesh, 
tree, and hybrid topologies, learning that each has advantages and disadvantages in terms of performance, reliability, cost, and ease of 
maintenance.

I learned about different communication models used in networking. In the client-server model, dedicated servers provide services while 
clients request them. This model offers centralized management, better security, and scalability. In contrast, the peer-to-peer (P2P) 
model allows devices to communicate directly with one another without relying on a central server, making it simpler but less suitable for 
large organizations.

Finally, I learned how a web request works. When I enter a website such as Google into my browser, the browser first finds the website's 
IP address using DNS. The request then travels from my computer to my router, through my ISP, across several routers on the internet, 
until it reaches Google's servers. The server processes the request, sends the webpage back through the network, and my browser displays 
the results—all within a matter of seconds.

Key Concepts I Must Remember

One of the most important distinctions is between LAN and WAN. A LAN is designed for communication within a limited area and typically 
offers high speeds because the devices are close together. A WAN, however, connects multiple LANs across long distances and forms the 
backbone of the global internet.

I must also clearly understand the difference between a router, switch, and modem. A router connects different networks and determines the 
best path for data to travel. A switch connects devices within the same LAN and forwards data only to the intended device. A modem 
converts signals so my local network can communicate with my ISP and access the internet.

The client-server model should also be clear in my mind. Clients initiate requests, while servers receive those requests, process them, 
and return the required information or service.

I also need to remember the three communication methods:

Unicast: One sender communicates with one receiver.
Broadcast: One sender communicates with every device on the local network.
Multicast: One sender communicates with a selected group of devices.
Real-World Understanding

From my understanding, the internet is not a single computer or server but a vast collection of interconnected networks spread across the 
world. Every time I visit a website, send a message, stream a video, or use an online application, my device creates data requests that 
travel through my router, to my ISP, and across many interconnected routers before reaching the destination server. That server processes 
my request and sends the response back through the same network path. Routers, switches, cables, wireless signals, and servers work 
together continuously to ensure information reaches the correct destination quickly and accurately. This process happens so fast that 
users experience it almost instantly.
